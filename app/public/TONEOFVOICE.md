# Fenster Tone Of Voice

Last updated: 2026-07-21

This is the copy voice reference for every customer-facing page. It was reverse-engineered from the About page (`template-parts\sections\about.php`), which the owner named as the best copy on the site. When writing or rewriting any page copy, match this voice. `STYLE.md` still owns the mechanical copy rules (we/you voice, no em dashes, plain CTA labels); this document explains how the good copy actually sounds and how to reproduce it.

## The Voice In One Line

A straight-talking local tradesperson who respects your intelligence, proves everything with specifics, and says the awkward thing before you have to ask.

## Where It Comes From

The About page opens with:

> Fenster exists to make windows and doors straightforward: a fair price you can get in minutes, people who know what they are fitting, and a company that is still here long after the scaffolding has gone.

That sentence carries every trait of the voice: a plain promise, a concrete triad instead of adjectives, and a small piece of real-world texture ("after the scaffolding has gone") instead of marketing abstraction. Everything below is a breakdown of how to keep doing that.

## Core Principles

### 1. Facts do the persuading, not adjectives

The copy never says "premium", "high-quality", "stunning" or "market-leading". It gives the fact and lets the reader draw the conclusion.

- Yes: "We started in 2018, our showroom is in Milton Keynes, and everyone who surveys, fits and answers the phone works for us."
- Yes: "In August 2025 it was ours: a Northampton extension with an S1 roof lantern overhead and black steel-look heritage doors to the garden. Johnnie and Tom fitted it. This is the actual job."
- No: "Our award-winning team delivers exceptional installations across the region."

If a sentence would survive on a competitor's website unchanged, it is too generic. Add the name, the place, the number or the date that makes it only true of Fenster.

### 2. Say the awkward thing first

The voice earns trust by volunteering the caveat or naming the industry's bad habit directly, before the customer has to dig for it.

- "Any installer can print a logo, so here is what each of ours covers, including the limits most keep in the small print."
- "One number for the job, not an online teaser and a different figure at the door."
- "No stock photos and no showroom sets."

The pattern is: state the suspicion a sensible customer already has, then answer it plainly. Never pretend the suspicion does not exist. Never over-promise to make it go away.

**Bounded by an owner instruction, 2026-08-02: do not write copy that states what is _not_ covered or _not_ included.** The site avoids that kind of negative. This principle is about candour on things a customer will meet in practice, not about listing exclusions, and the two used to be blurred here: an earlier version of this section used the guarantee limits as its worked example, which is exactly the copy the owner does not want. Prefer the positive statement that happens to be accurate. The order process rail is the model: it says the ten year guarantee covers "new windows and doors", which is true everywhere it renders and names no exclusion.

**Still live and unchanged: the `/about/` page states the guarantee exclusions at length**, and the product hub FAQs carry a guarantee-limits answer. Both predate this instruction and are owner-approved copy on the page the owner rates most. They have deliberately not been rewritten off the back of a note about one FAQ; raise it with the owner before touching them.

**And a second boundary, owner correction 2026-08-12, given for at least the third time: DO NOT SELL BY DESCRIBING WHAT GOES WRONG.** This is a different failure from the exclusions rule above and it is the one that keeps coming back. Writing about leaks, failures, things being rebuilt, callbacks or units coming back is fear-framing, not candour, and on a commercial page it is also faintly insulting to a reader who prices this work for a living. Caught and removed on the commercial rebuild: *"Head, cill, jamb and drainage decide whether it leaks"*, *"where a badly detailed one fails"*, *"a closer that will not hold a door in wind"*, *"get them wrong and the entrance is rebuilt"*, *"most of what goes wrong was visible on day one and nobody looked"*.

**The test that separates the two.** Principle 2 is about a caveat *the customer will meet in dealing with us* — a lead time, a limit, something awkward about our own service. It is not licence to describe the ways a building can fail. **State what the detail achieves, not what its absence causes.** "Head, cill, jamb and drainage are where a facade is made watertight" carries exactly the same specification and sells the care instead of the catastrophe.

### 2b. Abstraction is not expertise, and a trade word does not make it one

**Owner correction, 2026-08-12, on a sentence he called AI nonsense:** *"Taking it
as one package is the point, because one contractor then owns the joints between
the frame, the glass and the hardware, and they get settled once."* Frames, glass
and hardware have no joints between them. The sentence was shaped like a technical
benefit and contained nothing a reader could check, picture or act on.

**The tell is a word you reach for when you have not got a concrete thing to say.**
On that pass it was "interfaces": four uses across the commercial set and only one
of them naming an actual junction detail. The other three were doing the work a
real fact should have done — *"the interfaces are the part worth getting right"*,
*"keeps the interfaces and the performance sensible"*, *"one set of interfaces"*.
Watch for the same shape around **package, solution, coordination, alignment,
seamless, holistic, end to end, sensible, properly, robust**.

**The test, and it is the same one principle 1 gives:** can the reader check it,
picture it, or do something with it? "One survey, one order, one team on site" and
"the people who measure it are the people who fit it" pass. "One set of interfaces"
does not. If a sentence would survive on a competitor's site unchanged, it is
generic; if it would survive on a competitor's site *and nobody could tell what it
meant*, it is worse than generic.

**Where this comes from is worth knowing.** It is the same fault as writing the
page by adding to it: on that route the idea "we do it all as one package" was
stated four times in different words before anyone noticed none of them said how.
Say the concrete thing once.

### 3. Respect the reader's way of doing things

Routes and choices are presented as genuine equals, framed around how the customer prefers to operate, never mocking the alternative.

- "If you like doing things yourself, build the job on our quote tool... If you would rather talk it through, book a consultation."
- "Price it online, or let us come to you."

Use the "If you like X... If you would rather Y..." construction when the page offers two routes. Both branches get equal warmth.

### 4. Real people, real places, real jobs

Proof is always specific and human. First names of fitters, the town, the month, what was actually fitted.

- "Johnnie and Tom fitted it."
- "Aluminium bifold doors, Whitehouse, Milton Keynes."
- "Recent jobs, photographed the day we finished."
- "Behind them is a team from the trade, not a call centre."

When a claim can be tied to a named case study, person or accreditation page, tie it. When it cannot, ask whether the claim should be made at all.

### 5. Dry, light humour in small doses

One quiet aside per page at most, never in a heading that carries the sale, never forced.

- "Meet the team. Everyone here, including the office cat."
- "ask the awkward questions"
- "The name is the German word for window."

The humour is deadpan and factual, not jokey. If it needs an exclamation mark, cut it.

### 6. Physical, sensory verbs

When describing products or the showroom, use verbs of handling and doing, not verbs of admiring.

- "Come and open a bifold, feel the weight of a handle, compare a foil to a powder coat."
- No: "Explore our stunning range of premium hardware."

## Sentence And Rhythm Rules

- Short declarative sentences are the default. Vary length by occasionally following two short sentences with one longer one that lands a triad.
- The signature rhythm is the concrete triad: three parallel, specific items. "Your sizes, your finishes, a real figure in minutes." "Colours, glass, frames and handles never look quite the same on a screen." Use it for leads and closers, not every sentence.
- Sentence fragments are allowed when they land a point: "This is the actual job." "No waiting a week to find out the number."
- Ordinary punctuation only. No em dashes, no exclamation marks, no ellipses for drama. Colons are fine for setting up a list or expansion.
- Spell out small numbers in prose ("ten minutes", "ten year guarantee"); use figures for stats and data ("2018", "1,000+ installations").
- British English throughout.
- Write from Fenster to the customer: we, our, you. Never "the company", "your local specialist" or Fenster in the third person.

## Headings

Headings are complete customer-truth statements in sentence case, ending with a full stop. They tell the reader something useful, not something clever.

Real examples from the About page:

- "Simple, honest glazing."
- "Price it online, or let us come to you."
- "Recent jobs, photographed the day we finished."
- "Run by the two people who started it."
- "The badges, and what they actually mean."
- "See it all in person at the showroom."
- "Whatever you came here to work out."

Rules:

- Sentence case, closing full stop.
- The heading should still be worth reading if it were the only text on the page.
- Never use campaign headings: "Designed around you", "Explore the possibilities", "The perfect finishing touch", "Everything you need to know".
- Eyebrow labels above headings are short, plain noun phrases: "How pricing works", "Who runs it", "Our work", "Come and see us", "Where to next". No cleverness in eyebrows.

## CTAs And Buttons

- Buttons state the action in plain words: "Get an instant price", "Book a consultation", "See the project", "Meet the team", "Call 01908 429200".
- Never invented journey language: no "Plan my door", "Start your journey", "Discover the difference".
- Supporting copy under a CTA link is one specific sentence: "Your sizes, your finishes, a real figure in minutes." Not a slogan.

## Words And Phrases

Use naturally:

- straightforward, honest, real, actual, proper, awkward questions, on the spot, the trade, fitted, measured, the job, the number, the price

Never use:

- premium, luxury, stunning, bespoke solutions, market-leading, unrivalled, hassle-free, dream home, transform, elevate, discover, journey, perfect, exceptional, state-of-the-art
- superlative claims that cannot be substantiated
- supplier brochure phrasing of any kind (see below)
- **any wording that positions one of our own products as the weaker choice on specification.** Owner instruction, 2026-07-24. A comparison between two things we both sell is a comparison, not a verdict: give each the thing it is genuinely best at, in parallel phrasing, and let the figures separate them. "uPVC reaches the lower U-value, aluminium holds the slimmest frame" is right. "Aluminium is not the warmer of the two" is not, even though it is true.
- **"light" for a window or an opener.** Owner instruction, 2026-08-12. A fixed pane is a fixed pane, an opener is an opener or a sash, and a small window is a window. "A three light" and "a bathroom light" are trade shorthand that a homeowner either misreads as lighting or does not read at all. **`toplight`, `sidelight` and `leaded light` are the exceptions and only for what they actually name** — the small window above a door, the one beside it, and a leaded glass panel. The first two name a component a customer is quoted for; the third names the glass rather than the opening. Full rule, including the replacements and the `roof light` exception, in `AI.md`.
- **budget, cheap, or any wording that positions one of our own products as the low-cost option.** Owner instruction, 2026-07-24, after a product hub card led with "Folding, on a budget". Every product we sell is one we would fit on our own house, and framing one as the compromise sells against it. This covers the softer forms too: "at a lower cost than", "warmer for the money", "costs less". Say what the product *does* differently instead. Slide and fold doors are not a cheaper bifold, they are doors whose panels each open on their own. Prices belong on the price guides and the quote tool, where they come with a real figure.

## Rewriting Supplier / Scrape Content

Source material from manufacturer scrapes (Distinction, Roseview, Liniar, Sheerline) is fact material only. The facts are useful; the voice is not.

Supplier copy sounds like: "Our extensive range of high-performance composite doors combines stunning aesthetics with market-leading security." That must never survive into Fenster copy, even lightly edited.

The process:

1. Extract the verifiable facts: dimensions, materials, glass names, security standards, test results, colour counts.
2. Check each fact against what Fenster actually sells (see the confirmed-facts sections in `AI.md` and page-specific docs). Do not carry over ranges, options or claims Fenster does not offer.
3. Rewrite from scratch as Fenster speaking to a customer, using the principles above. Lead with what the fact means for the customer's house, not the manufacturer's pride.

Example transformation:

- Supplier: "Our superior GRP doors have a high impact resistant skin with a beautiful woodgrain finish which gives an authentic and premium aesthetic."
- Fenster: "The skin is GRP, the same tough material boat hulls are made from, moulded with a woodgrain you have to touch to know is not timber."

## Page-Level Structure

The About page also shows how the voice structures a page:

- Open with the plainest possible statement of what the page is about, then the concrete triad.
- One idea per section. Each section heading is a truth, the body proves it with specifics, and it ends before it repeats itself.
- Caveats and limits appear in the body of the relevant section, not in a footnote.
- End with practical routes onward, described in one plain line each.

## Self-Check Before Shipping Copy

Read the drafted copy and ask:

1. Could a competitor paste this unchanged onto their site? If yes, it lacks specifics.
2. Does any sentence exist only to sound good? Cut it.
3. Is there a caveat or awkward truth the customer will eventually discover? Say it now, plainly.
4. Do the headings end with a full stop and state something useful?
5. Are there any banned words, em dashes, exclamation marks or third-person Fenster references?
6. Does at least one section carry real proof: a name, a place, a date, a number?
7. Read it aloud. If it does not sound like a knowledgeable fitter explaining something at your kitchen table, rewrite it until it does.
